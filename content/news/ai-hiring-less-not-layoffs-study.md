The fear most people have about AI at work is a layoff notice. A study published on November 23 points somewhere else: the job that opens up when someone leaves and never gets posted.

## Who did the study

It comes from Anthropic, the company that makes Claude, an AI assistant that competes with ChatGPT. Its researchers interviewed people at 58 US companies that use Claude. The Decoder, a technology news site, reports there were 66 in-person interviews, at firms in law, construction, finance, medicine and manufacturing as well as AI startups.

Keep the limits in mind. Every company in the study is an Anthropic customer, and The Decoder notes the authors say the sample isn't representative. It also comes from a company that sells AI. I read it as a close look at 58 workplaces, which is useful, and no more than that.

## What they found about jobs

- People at only two of the companies had seen AI fully eliminate a position and lead to someone losing their job.
- People at five companies described pushing out employees who would not adopt AI.
- People at two-thirds of the companies reported hiring less because of AI. Some reported hiring more.
- Nine firms cut outside contractors, mostly offshore work in software development, customer support, design and legal services, according to The Decoder.

## What it feels like inside those companies

The report is frank about the mood. The Decoder counts employee resistance as the second most common obstacle companies mentioned. One worker told the researchers, "If I'm open to this, I'm essentially building my own coffin."

It is also frank that AI often doesn't pay off. Respondents said 55% of the AI projects studied produced more output or better quality, and 19% delivered no business value. The most common complaint was the volume of AI-written material that a person still has to check.

## What I'd do with this

It fits what I'd expect from four years of building these systems. A small business rarely lets someone go because of software. More often a person leaves, the owner looks at the list of what that person did, and some of it goes to AI while the remainder goes to the team. The posting never goes up.

If you're an employee, the practical point is that the risk sits with the next opening more than with your current seat. In this study, more companies pushed out people who refused the tools than cut a job outright. Learning to use AI on the repeatable parts of your work, and being the person who checks what it produces, is the safer position.

If you're the parent of someone about to enter the job market, this is the harder news. Fewer openings hurt people trying to get a first job more than people who already have one. An applicant who can show they've used AI on real work, and caught its mistakes, has something to point to.

If you own a business, be honest with your team about which duties are moving and what happens when a seat opens. The Decoder's account says pushback was most visible where companies rolled AI out from the top to automate whole workflows. People cooperate with a plan they can see.
