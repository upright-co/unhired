The next time you are in an Alberta emergency department, the doctor may ask whether an AI can listen to your conversation. Here is what that request is about.

## What Jenkins is

Jenkins is an AI scribe, software that listens as a doctor speaks with a patient and helps with the notes for the chart. The University of Alberta reported in March 2026 that it is being tested in 81 emergency departments across the province. It launched in November 2024.

The university says the tool uses large language models, the kind of technology behind AI assistants like ChatGPT, to transcribe a conversation and summarize what it means. **Patients must consent to its use.**

## The numbers so far

As of the university's March update:

- 373 physicians had completed training and were actively using it.
- They had recorded nearly 31,000 clinical sessions, for a total of 3,000 hours of audio.
- 30 family physicians were testing it as well.

## Where it came from

This one is homegrown. It was first created by Michael Weldon, an emergency doctor and engineer based in Red Deer. It was developed further by Jake Hayward, an emergency physician and assistant clinical professor at the University of Alberta, and his team at the university's AI + Health Hub. Hayward is also deputy clinical head of quality improvement with Alberta Health Services.

"Broad adoption isn't just about handing out software," Hayward said. "It requires intentional support and integration with existing electronic health records."

The university says the project is funded through the Health Care Unburdened Grant program, from the Canadian Medical Association, MD Financial Management and Scotiabank.

## What we don't know yet

The university team says it is beginning to analyze the data to understand the tool's cost-effectiveness, its effect on clinical outcomes and whether it lowers stress for doctors. Until that work is published, nobody can say with numbers whether it shortens waits. I'd treat any claim that it does as a hope for now.

## Why I think it is a sensible use of AI

I've built AI systems for businesses for four years, and note-taking is one of the jobs AI does well. A doctor who is typing is looking at a screen. A doctor who isn't typing can look at you.

The safe way to run a tool like this is for the AI to draft and the doctor to read and approve every note. That is the first thing I'd ask about, because these tools are fast and usually right, and sometimes they write down something nobody said.

## What to ask if you're the patient

You are allowed to ask questions before you say yes. I'd ask three. Is the recording kept after the note is written, and for how long? Does the doctor review the note before it goes in my chart? Can I say no without it affecting my care? A good program will have quick answers to all three.
