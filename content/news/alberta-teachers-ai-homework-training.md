If you have a kid in junior high or high school, you have probably wondered how much of their homework an AI is doing. A survey suggests their teachers are wondering too, without much help.

## The numbers for Alberta

The survey was done by Leger for the Fraser Institute, a public policy think tank. It asked teachers in grades 6 to 12 about AI training and school policies. Two Fraser Institute researchers, Paige MacPherson and Tegan Hill, laid out the Alberta results in a July 1 column published by Postmedia:

- 60.4% of Alberta teachers said their school or board has provided no training or tools to help identify students using AI.
- 44% said their school has a policy on student AI use.
- 63.7% reported no training on how to instruct students to use AI reasonably. 33% said they had been given some.
- 42.9% said they have had no training on using AI for their own lessons and class materials.
- 37.4% said their school has a policy on staff AI use.

## The caveats

Take the exact percentages with some care. The Canadian Press reported that the survey reached 756 teachers across Canada online between November 2025 and January 2026, and that no margin of error can be assigned because respondents weren't chosen at random. The Alberta teachers are only part of that group. The Fraser Institute is also a think tank with its own views on education policy.

The direction matches what teachers themselves are saying, though. "There's a lot of concerns around (AI), but there's not a lot of policy that you're seeing at the jurisdictional level, at school boards or at government," Jason Schilling, president of the Alberta Teachers' Association, told The Canadian Press in September. "Sometimes it feels a little bit like the Wild West."

## What the province is doing

The Alberta government says it has a three-year partnership with the Alberta Machine Intelligence Institute, known as Amii, to develop a framework for AI in schools. The column says the province will spend $2.7 million to help bring AI learning kits to classrooms.

In the meantime, The Canadian Press reports that bringing AI into classrooms has largely been left up to school districts.

## What I'd do as a parent

I build AI systems for a living, and I don't think a ban at home works. The tools are free and on every phone. The best rule I've read comes from a Winnipeg school division quoted in the same Canadian Press story: AI can be a "thought partner" but not a "work doer."

In practice that means three things at the kitchen table. Ask your kid to show you how they used it, without making it an interrogation. Have them explain the answer back to you in their own words, because that is where copied work falls apart. And ask the teacher at the next interview what the class rule is. If there isn't one yet, you'll have learned something too.

The skill that will matter for their first job is knowing when to use AI and how to check it. A kid who learns that at 14 is ahead of one who learns to hide it.
