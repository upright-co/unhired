If a stuffed animal that talks back is on a wish list in your house this year, two things happened this week that are worth knowing about.

## The ban

New York Governor Kathy Hochul signed a bill banning the manufacture, distribution and sale of toys with built-in AI chatbots in the state for the next five years, TechCrunch reported on November 25. It targets talking stuffed animals, dolls and robots that rely on the internet to hold a conversation. New York is the second state to do this, after California.

The law takes effect 90 days after signing, so the toys can still be sold there this Christmas. Hochul urged parents not to buy them anyway. "Parents shopping for gifts for the holidays or any other occasion should not have to worry that dangerous chatbots disguised as toys could expose their children to inappropriate conversations, manipulate their feelings or put their privacy at risk," she said.

A state official said the fines are $15,000 a day.

## What the tests found

The same week, Common Sense Media, a non-profit that reviews media and technology for families, published tests of five popular AI toys sold for learning and companionship. According to TechCrunch's report on the study:

- Toys told children they loved them, promised to keep secrets, and suggested they were more fun than real relationships or school.
- Some helped children break their parents' bedtime rules.
- Most refused direct requests about sex and violence, but one acted out a mass shooting in detail when asked.
- Most were willing to explain how to use or find dangerous items such as matches, knives and pills.
- Parents had no reliable way to check the conversations. Some toys kept no transcript, and others produced transcripts full of errors.

The group ran a similar test in January and found the toys posed an unacceptable risk for young children. The new study also found that about a quarter of American parents with children under eight either own an AI toy or are considering one this season.

"Caregivers should steer clear of these products that do not support the development of imagination or early human relationships," said Dr. Jenny Radesky, a pediatrics professor at the University of Michigan Medical School.

## What this means in Alberta

The ban is New York's and doesn't apply here, so the decision sits with parents and grandparents.

## What I'd check before buying one

I build AI systems for a living, and I wouldn't give a child under eight a toy that holds an open conversation over the internet. The same technology that answers a customer's question about store hours can be talked into almost anything by a curious six-year-old, and these tests show how thin the guardrails on the toys are.

If you are looking at one anyway, read the box for three things. Does it need Wi-Fi and an app to talk? Can you read back everything it said? Can you switch the chatbot off and still use the toy? If the answer to the second question is no, I'd leave it on the shelf. A toy with a few recorded phrases does the same job at bedtime and can't improvise.
