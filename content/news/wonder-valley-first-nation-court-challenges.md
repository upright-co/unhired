Most data centre talk in Alberta this month is about Bill 42. Meanwhile Wonder Valley, the $70 billion campus Kevin O'Leary wants to build south of Grande Prairie, is now facing three court challenges from the First Nation whose territory it sits in.

## The project

Wonder Valley was first announced in December 2024. It is planned to draw 7.5 gigawatts of power. CTV News, using figures from Alberta's grid operator, says that is more than five times Edmonton's electricity demand. The developer is O'Leary Digital.

## The three challenges

Sturgeon Lake Cree Nation, which has about 4,000 members, has filed three judicial reviews. A judicial review asks a court to decide whether a government decision was made properly.

- The first, filed last year, challenges the water licence granted for the project. CTV reported on November 3 that it is set to be heard next month.
- The second, filed in September, challenges the province's decision to exempt Wonder Valley from an environmental impact assessment. The filing calls that decision "irrational" and says it breaches Alberta's constitutional obligations.
- The third, filed in October, says the province consulted at the "lowest possible level" and gave the Nation 15 working days to respond to the project plans.

The filings say the Nation holds traplines and rights across the Treaty 8 territory where the campus would be built.

In August, a judge dismissed O'Leary Digital's attempt to have the water licence case thrown out on procedural grounds.

## What each side says

Chief Sheldon Sunshine told CTV News Edmonton the Nation has been kept in the dark and that going to court is the only way to get answers. He named water use in a region already dealing with drought, and the heat a 7.5 gigawatt facility gives off. "It's going to have significant impacts on our way of life," he said.

O'Leary Digital CEO Paul Palandjian said in a statement that the reviews are aimed at the government and that the company respects the Nation's right to file them. The company says Wonder Valley is not exempt from environmental review or permits, and that it has committed to closed-loop cooling, which cuts water demand. It plans to file an application with the Alberta Utilities Commission by the end of 2026 and does not expect the court cases to change its timeline.

The province said it can't comment while the matter is before the courts.

## Why it matters to you

Bill 42, tabled on November 18, sets new rules on where big data centres can go. Projects that already have approvals are exempt. So the rules that decide how Wonder Valley gets built are the ones already in place, and the courts are now testing whether those rules were followed.

University of Calgary law professor David Wright told CTV that environmental assessments for data centres could prevent this kind of litigation. I think he's right. A full review takes longer up front, but it gives everyone, including the company, a clear answer on water and power before billions are spent. The water licence hearing next month is the date I'd watch.
