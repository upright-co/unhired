At 7:50 on a Tuesday, a vet clinic's phone already has four voicemails. A boarding kennel wants a rabies certificate. A client is confirming that their dog had nothing to eat after midnight before today's dental, and someone else needs a refill of their cat's thyroid medication. The last voicemail is a man whose Lab got into a bag of sugar-free gum an hour ago.

That last call is the reason I'd set up a vet front desk differently from almost any other. I've spent the last four years building AI systems and agents for businesses, and the volume work at a vet clinic is a very good fit for an AI Receptionist. The calls mixed in with it are not, and the whole setup depends on telling them apart.

This post covers what an AI Receptionist does at a veterinary clinic, what it should always pass to your team, a normal day, and what it costs next to a hire.

## What an AI Receptionist does at a vet clinic

An AI Receptionist is an [AI Employee](/blog/what-is-an-ai-employee) that holds the front-desk job. It has a manager (usually the practice manager), logins to the phone line, texting, email and the practice management system, and written rules about what it can do on its own. Every call and message goes into a log the practice manager can read.

The general version of the job is in the post on the [AI Receptionist](/blog/ai-receptionist). At a vet clinic, five duties take most of the hours.

### Reminders and confirmations

Vaccines, heartworm tests, dental cleanings and senior wellness exams all come due on a schedule, and the reminder list is the first thing to slip when the phones are busy. An AI Receptionist works it every day. It texts or emails each client whose pet is due, offers a couple of open times, books whoever answers and follows up with the ones who don't.

It also sends tomorrow's confirmations and handles the replies. When a 10 am appointment cancels, it offers the slot to clients on the short-notice list.

One rule matters more here than anywhere else: when a pet has died, reminders stop that day. A "Bella is due for her rabies booster" text to a family that put Bella down last week is the kind of mistake people remember. The AI Receptionist checks the record before every reminder and never sends one to a deceased patient.

### Surgery and procedure prep

Every spay, neuter, dental and mass removal comes with instructions: no food after midnight, water is fine until morning, drop-off between 7:30 and 8, bring any current medications, plan to pick up after 3. The AI Receptionist sends them two days before, confirms the night before, and answers "can she have water?" at 9 pm from the clinic's written instructions.

When a client says the dog already ate breakfast, it doesn't decide whether the surgery goes ahead. It flags the appointment for the surgical tech before the clinic opens.

### Records requests

Boarding kennels, groomers, daycares, trainers and other clinics ask for vaccine certificates and records constantly. The AI Receptionist checks that the request comes from the owner or has the owner's okay, pulls the certificate from the record and sends it. A request for full medical records from another hospital gets the same check, with the files sent the way the clinic allows.

### Refill requests

Long-term medications for thyroid conditions, arthritis, allergies and seizures make up a steady stream of calls. The AI Receptionist takes the request, checks the date of the pet's last exam and puts it in the veterinarian's queue with the drug, dose and pharmacy. It never approves a refill. When the vet signs off it tells the client when to pick up, and when the vet wants an exam first, it books one.

### After-hours and overflow calls

When both front-desk staff are on the phone, or the clinic is closed, calls go to the AI Receptionist instead of voicemail. It books, reschedules and answers from the clinic's own information: hours, which species you see, what a new patient visit costs, what to bring. It can also take pet insurance questions as far as which forms the clinic fills out and how long they take.

## What it should always hand to a person

A vet clinic needs a short list of calls that never stay with the AI Receptionist, and a fast path to a person for each one.

![The calls an AI Receptionist hands to a person at a vet clinic](/blog/ai-receptionist-for-veterinary-clinic/03-handoffs.jpg)

- Anything that might be an emergency. Trouble breathing, a swollen or hard belly, repeated vomiting, collapse, seizures, a pet hit by a car, a cat straining in the litter box, or anything the pet may have eaten that could be toxic (gum with xylitol, lilies, grapes, rat bait, someone's medication). The AI Receptionist gives the clinic's written instructions and alerts staff right away. After hours, that means the nearest emergency hospital and a poison line such as [ASPCA Animal Poison Control](https://www.aspca.org/pet-care/animal-poison-control). It never tells a client to wait and see.
- Questions about symptoms or medication. "Is it normal that he's limping after his walk?" goes to a technician or vet. The AI Receptionist books the earliest appropriate visit or a callback.
- End-of-life calls. Anyone asking about euthanasia, and anyone who is grieving, gets a person on the first sentence.
- Estimates and money. It can share published prices. Treatment estimates, payment plans and billing disputes go to whoever handles them.
- Upset clients, or anyone who asks for a person.

I'd write the emergency list with the medical director and keep it short and literal. The AI Receptionist should match a caller's words against it and pass on anything close. Being wrong in the cautious direction costs a technician two minutes.

Clients should also know who they're talking to. A good setup says it's an AI assistant for the clinic at the start of the call and offers a person whenever asked. The article on [whether customers should know they're talking to an AI Employee](/blog/ai-disclosure-customers) covers how to word it.

## What a vet clinic front-desk hire costs

The US Bureau of Labor Statistics puts the [median wage for receptionists](https://www.bls.gov/ooh/office-and-administrative-support/receptionists.htm) at $38,010 a year, or $18.27 an hour, as of May 2025.

Wages are only part of the cost. Across US private industry, wages and salaries are about 70% of what employers spend on compensation, according to the [BLS Employer Costs for Employee Compensation](https://www.bls.gov/news.release/ecec.nr0.htm) report. At that ratio, a receptionist at the median wage costs about $54,300 a year once benefits and payroll taxes are counted. Then come the job ad, interviews, training on the practice software, and covering lunches, sick days and vacation. Front-desk staff at vet clinics often move into technician or assistant roles, so many clinics run that hiring cycle more than once a year.

## How much you'd pay for an AI Receptionist at a vet clinic

There are two ways to get one.

You can assemble it yourself: an AI phone and chat tool, a connection to your practice management system, and a texting service, or the reminder tool you already use. The subscriptions are the smaller cost. The bigger one is the time someone spends mapping the call types, writing the booking rules and the emergency list, testing it and keeping it current.

Or a provider builds and maintains it, usually for a monthly fee. You pay more than the bare software, and someone else handles the setup, updates and log reviews.

The price moves with a few things: how many channels it covers (phone, text, email, web chat), whether your practice management system has a clean integration, call volume, how many doctors and locations it books for, whether you see exotics or large animals with their own rules, and how much maintenance is included.

To compare quotes, set them against the full cost of the seat: about $54,300 a year for one receptionist at the median wage, for about 40 hours of coverage a week. Vet clinics usually take calls for more hours than that.

## How an AI Receptionist is set up at a vet clinic

Setup looks like training a new front-desk hire, with the emergency rules written before anything else.

![How an AI Receptionist is set up at a veterinary clinic](/blog/ai-receptionist-for-veterinary-clinic/06-setup.jpg)

The step that decides whether it works is the shadow period. For the first week or two, the AI Receptionist drafts its replies and bookings while the practice manager reads the log, and it takes on more once it's getting them right. After that, someone still reviews the log every week. Clinics add a doctor, change surgery days, switch reminder tools and update prices, and an AI Receptionist nobody updates starts booking last quarter's schedule.

## Is your front desk a good fit?

If your front desk spends most of the day at the counter, checking in nervous dogs, taking payments and comforting clients after hard appointments, you still need a person there. An AI Receptionist can take the phones, reminders and records requests off their plate, which often makes the job manageable again.

If most of the desk's day is phones, texts, reminders and scheduling, an AI Receptionist can hold most of the seat, with a person at the counter and on the handoffs.

To check your own clinic, have the front desk tally every call and message for one ordinary week by type: reminder, confirmation, surgery question, records request, refill, new client, estimate, emergency, end of life. Then sort the types into three groups: what an AI Employee could own, what it could do with a person checking, and what needs someone at the desk. Some websites offer [free assessments](/assessment) that check whether a role like this can be filled by an AI Employee, which is a reasonable next step once you have the tally.
