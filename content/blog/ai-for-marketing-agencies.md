The first week of the month at a marketing agency belongs to reports. At an agency with thirty retainer clients, that's thirty rounds of logging in to Google Analytics, Google Ads and Meta Ads Manager, pulling last month's numbers into a Looker Studio page or a slide template, and writing a paragraph on what changed. While that's going on, a new client still hasn't granted access to their ad account, two clients are sitting on content that was due for approval last Thursday, and one campaign has spent 80% of its monthly budget by the 9th.

I've spent the last four years building AI systems and agents for businesses, and agencies split more cleanly than most. Clients pay for the ideas and the judgment of the people on their account. Around that sits account operations: the reports, logins, reminders, scheduling and hour tracking that keep thirty accounts moving, most of which no client is billed for.

This post covers what an AI Employee does at a marketing agency, what it should always leave to the team, what to read in your client contracts first, and how the cost compares to the hire it would replace.

## What account operations at an agency involves

Whether the agency runs paid media, SEO, social, email or all four, the same work sits around the client work. Somebody builds the reports, and somebody else spends a week getting a new client to hand over five logins. Approvals get chased, posts get scheduled, spend gets checked and hours get counted against the retainer. At a twelve-person agency this is usually split between an account coordinator, a project manager and whichever account manager has a spare hour.

An [AI Employee](/blog/what-is-an-ai-employee) in this role holds that job the way a coordinator would. It reports to the operations lead or an account director. It has its own seat in the project tool (Asana, ClickUp or whatever the agency runs), the reporting tool, the scheduler, the time tracker and the client email inbox, and it works under written rules about what it can do alone. Everything it does is logged against the client's account. My post on the [AI marketing assistant](/blog/ai-marketing-assistant) covers this seat inside a single business. An agency has the same job multiplied by every client, and it breaks down like this.

### Client reports

This is the duty I'd hand over first. For each client, the AI Employee pulls last month's numbers from the ad and analytics accounts, fills in the agency's template and drafts the commentary: which campaign's cost per lead moved, which landing page sent the most form fills, what the search terms report turned up. The draft lands with the account manager two days before it's due.

The account manager reads it, rewrites what needs their judgment and sends it. I wouldn't change that step, because the client is paying for that person's read on the numbers.

### Onboarding and access

A new client means a list: partner access in Meta Business Suite, a link request to the Google Ads account, a seat on the Google Analytics property, Search Console, the website's CMS, brand fonts, logo files and the intake questionnaire. The client's office manager usually has half of these and no idea where the rest are.

The AI Employee sends the list with step-by-step instructions for each item, checks every morning which ones have arrived, and follows up on the ones that haven't. When the file is complete, the account manager gets one message saying the account is ready to work on.

### Approvals and feedback

Content goes to the client on Tuesday for a Thursday approval, and Thursday passes. The AI Employee sends the reminders on a schedule the agency sets, and tells the account manager when a publish date is at risk. When the feedback does arrive, spread across five emails and a voicemail transcript, it combines everything into one numbered list on the task so the designer isn't piecing it together.

### Scheduling and trafficking

Once a post, email or ad is approved, getting it live is mechanical. The AI Employee schedules approved posts in the client's scheduler, adds tracking links, and checks the next morning that each one published. For ads, it uploads approved copy and images as paused drafts. The media buyer reviews and launches them.

### Daily account checks

Every morning it reads every client's ad accounts and analytics and looks for the problems that are expensive to find late: a campaign pacing to overspend its monthly budget, a disapproved ad, a landing page returning an error, conversion tracking that stopped recording on Saturday. It reports each one to the media buyer with the account and the numbers. It has read-only access here and changes nothing.

### Retainers, hours and billing

It compares the hours logged in Harvest or Toggl against each client's retainer and warns the account director when an account is on pace to run over, while there's still time to talk to the client. At month end it drafts the invoices, including ad spend the agency paid on the client's behalf, for someone in finance to approve. Chasing the unpaid ones is a job of its own, which I covered in the post on [AI accounts receivable](/blog/ai-accounts-receivable).

## What it should always hand to your team

Agency work goes out under someone else's name, so the list of things I'd keep with a person is longer than in most businesses.

- Strategy and creative. The AI Employee can gather the numbers and past work behind a recommendation, but the positioning and the concept come from your people.
- Budgets and launches. A person moves money and turns campaigns on.
- Anything published under a client's name that the client hasn't approved.
- Bad news. A missed target, a mistake on the account or a dip the client will ask about gets a call from the account manager.
- Scope and pricing. When a client asks for "one small extra thing", a person decides whether it's in the retainer. Renewal talks belong to the account director too.
- New claims for regulated clients. Clinics and law firms have advertising rules, and someone who knows them reads anything new.
- A client who is upset, or who asks for their account manager. That handoff happens on the first request.

## What to check in your client contracts first

An agency holds logins and data for dozens of other businesses, so your clients' contracts set the rules here more than your own preferences do. Before connecting anything, I'd read each one for two things: the confidentiality terms, and any clause about AI. More client contracts now say whether the agency may put client data into AI tools, and some require the agency to disclose it. If a contract is silent, I'd still tell the client what the AI Employee does on their account. The post on [whether customers should know they're talking to an AI Employee](/blog/ai-disclosure-customers) covers the wording.

Then there's the rule that matters most at an agency: one client's data never appears in another client's work. That covers customer lists, performance numbers, ad copy and search term data. Two of your clients may compete with each other, so the AI Employee should work on one account at a time with only that account's files in front of it.

The access rules I wouldn't bend: its own seat on the agency's manager accounts instead of someone's personal login, read-only on every ad account, no access to billing or payment methods, client data only with AI vendors whose terms say it isn't used to train their models, and every action logged. The post on [AI Employee data security](/blog/ai-employee-data-security) covers the general version.

## What the seat costs a person

Agencies usually put an account coordinator or a project manager on this work. The US Bureau of Labor Statistics groups coordinators with [market research analysts and marketing specialists](https://www.bls.gov/ooh/business-and-financial/market-research-analysts.htm), whose median wage was $78,760 a year ($37.87 an hour) in May 2025. The median for [project management specialists](https://www.bls.gov/ooh/business-and-financial/project-management-specialists.htm) was $102,320, or $49.19 an hour.

Wages are only part of it. Across US private industry, wages and salaries were 70% of what employers spent on compensation in June 2026, according to the [BLS Employer Costs for Employee Compensation](https://www.bls.gov/news.release/ecec.nr0.htm) report. At that ratio a coordinator at the median costs about $112,500 a year and a project manager about $146,200.

![What an agency operations seat costs each year](/blog/ai-for-marketing-agencies/05-cost.jpg)

Agencies feel this cost differently from most businesses, because very little of the work can be billed. A retainer pays for results, and no client accepts a line item for four approval reminders. The hours come straight out of the margin on the account.

## How much you'd pay for an AI Employee at a marketing agency

You can assemble one from software: an AI agent platform, connections to your reporting tool, project tool, scheduler and time tracker, and read-only access to the ad platforms. The subscriptions are the smaller cost. The bigger one is someone's time writing the report template, the reminder schedules and the handoff rules, testing them on real accounts and keeping them current when a platform changes its reporting.

Or a provider builds and maintains it, usually for a monthly fee. You pay more than the bare software, and someone else handles the setup and the upkeep.

The price moves with the number of client accounts, how many platforms each one runs on, how often reports go out, and how much maintenance is included. An SEO agency sending one monthly report per client needs less than a paid media agency checking forty ad accounts every morning.

To compare quotes, set them against the full cost of the seat: about $112,500 a year for a coordinator at the US median, or the unbilled hours your account managers log on reports and reminders now.

## How an AI Employee is set up at an agency

Setup looks a lot like onboarding a new coordinator, with the contract review at the start.

![How an AI Employee is set up at a marketing agency](/blog/ai-for-marketing-agencies/06-setup.jpg)

The report template takes the most thought. Most agencies I've seen have one template on paper and a different version in every account manager's head, including which numbers each client cares about and which ones start an argument. Writing that down per client is useful even without an AI Employee, because it's usually why one account manager's reports take an hour and another's take four.

For the first few weeks, the AI Employee drafts every report and client message, and someone reads each one before it goes out. It takes on more once the drafts stop needing edits. After that, someone still reads the log weekly, because platforms rename metrics and a rule written in spring can be wrong by fall.

## Is your agency a good fit?

If you're a small studio with a handful of clients, and the partners talk to every one of them every week, the AI Employee has less to do. Those conversations are probably why the clients stay.

If your account managers lose the first week of every month to reports, and the project manager spends more time chasing logins and approvals than planning work, the operations seat is a strong candidate. Some websites offer [free assessments](/assessment) that check whether a role like this can be filled by an AI Employee.

To check your own agency, export last month's timesheets and tag every entry that wasn't strategy, creative or a client conversation: report, access, approval chasing, scheduling, account check, billing. Add up the hours under each tag. If reports are the biggest number, start by writing down one client's report exactly the way the account manager builds it now, because every later step of the setup works from that document.
